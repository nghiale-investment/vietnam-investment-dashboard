# Executive Summary

FPT reported a better second quarter after a softer start to 2026. For 1H2026, management cited consolidated revenue growth of 12.6% and profit-after-tax growth of about 14%. Q2 revenue grew 16.4% and profit before tax grew 20%. Technology remained the principal engine: 1H technology revenue and profit grew about 15% and 17%, respectively, while Q2 technology revenue and profit grew about 19% and 23.2%. Global IT services accelerated to 16.3% revenue growth in Q2. Japan remained strongest at about 22% reported growth, the U.S. grew about 6%, Europe about 40%, and APAC remained weak because of South Korea and Malaysia.

Bookings were materially stronger than recognized revenue. FPT signed 14 overseas contracts worth more than USD10 million each in 1H2026, up about 27% year on year. Total global IT signed revenue was close to USD1 billion, up 32.3%; management said like-for-like growth excluding longer-duration infrastructure and systems-integration commitments was still close to 20%. By market, signed revenue grew more than 40% in Japan, about 20% in the U.S., and more than 50% in Europe, while APAC declined by a single-digit rate. AIX transformation work was said to be growing three to four times faster than traditional IT services, but assessment and reverse-engineering phases delay the revenue mix shift by six to nine months before larger migration teams are deployed.

Management presented FPT’s strategic shift from digital transformation to AI transformation through three layers: the CASAN maturity and transformation-roadmap framework, the FleziPT transformation ecosystem, and Digital Foundry for scalable delivery. FPT said every FPT Software engineer is now AI-augmented, using at least one AI assistant or AI platform. The next workforce objective is to develop more than 1,000 people in a new AI/deployment-engineer category during the next 12 months, although the precise role name was unclear in the recording. FPT stressed model independence: it uses powerful models for new or difficult work, then specializes or substitutes cheaper models and reusable internal assets to reduce recurring cost.

The July operating update contained several potentially important disclosures. Management said FPT had become an OpenAI partner and expected a public announcement, with an initial focus on cybersecurity. A new service referred to as “Patch the Enterprise” would use OpenAI models to identify and patch security vulnerabilities in legacy systems, including systems built and operated by other vendors. FPT said a Japanese systems-integration partner had already brought an approximately USD1 million initial deal and targeted more than USD10 million of revenue from the offering in 2H2026. The exact product name and partnership scope require confirmation.

In Japan, FPT won a legacy-modernization engagement for the accounting system of a top-three automaker and described a changing relationship with local systems integrators. Rather than operating only as a subcontractor, FPT said it would act as prime contractor while Hitachi supported PMO work; FPT would provide assessment, migration, testing, methodology, and AI-powered tools. Management said Hitachi was close to finalizing a strategic alliance with FPT, complementing existing relationships with SCSK and NEC. FPT also hosted more than 300 Japanese customers at an AIX event in Hạ Long in early July. Outside Japan, a major U.S. renewable-energy client was expanding FPT’s relationship from one subsidiary to four, with management seeing scope to double the account in 2H2026 and again in 2027. Middle East disruption was described as both a risk and an opening because incumbent vendors were reluctant to deploy personnel, allowing FPT to bid on larger transformation programs.

Commercial models are moving away from named-person time-and-materials contracts toward fixed capacity by sprint and outcome-based pricing. Management wants eventually to convert all traditional named-resource T&M work to fixed-capacity or outcome-based arrangements. This allows FPT to combine people and agents flexibly, relocate mature work from tier-one cities to lower-cost delivery locations, release senior employees for higher-value assignments, and retain productivity gains. Management said large clients usually have backlogs and have not reduced FPT work because AI raises productivity; many instead order more work while keeping teams stable. However, some clients remain reluctant to make long commitments because model capabilities and economics change every three to six months.

The two AI Factory clusters in Vietnam and Japan were described as fully booked and operating, at roughly USD50 million of investment each. They reached breakeven during Q2, so 1H bottom-line contribution was limited, but the Vietnam cluster materially boosted domestic IT profit. Management cited about USD12 million of AI Factory revenue in 1H2026 and an annual revenue capacity of roughly USD25-30 million for the existing clusters. Future expansion will be customer-led and require guaranteed demand rather than speculative build-out. Data-sovereignty rules may favor local-country deployments; Europe and APAC had been discussed, but no next location was selected.

Domestic IT revenue grew more than 20%, and management indicated Q2 bottom-line growth was approximately double year on year, supported by the Vietnam AI Factory. Work with state-owned enterprises and the public sector was expected to sustain revenue growth through year-end. Education revenue declined 6.2%; management expected to have clearer full-year enrollment visibility in October.

Capital allocation drew scrutiny. Management said FPT had internally discussed a share buyback following the sector and stock-price decline and might act “at the right time” to protect shareholder value, but made no commitment. Separately, an investor described the proposed Hanoi technology park as a USD2 billion project. Management did not explicitly validate that figure but said the site would be developed over five to ten years and include a software campus, data centers, semiconductor initiatives, and lower-cost employee housing. FPT argued that government-supported land and replacement of rented offices for nearly 10,000 Hanoi employees could make the project economically attractive. Funding mix, phasing, returns, and cash-flow effects were not disclosed.

Tax and workforce changes were also discussed. Management attributed the higher 1H effective tax rate to a roughly USD2 million Japanese tax refund in the prior-year base, expiration of a tax holiday at one Vietnam software site, and weaker education profit, which normally carries a low tax rate. The gap between PBT and PAT growth was expected to narrow from about four percentage points in 1H to about two points for the full year, with PAT growth targeted near 15%. FPT Software’s trailing-12-month attrition was about 11%. Management said revenue-producing headcount increased by about 800, while back-office vacancies generally would not be replaced as internal AI raised productivity; it emphasized natural attrition rather than layoffs.

FPT Telecom continued to grow broadband and corporate services, while pay-TV profitability was pressured by higher content-license investment. Management cited revenue growth of about 15.5% and profit growth of about 13.8%, and said FPT had no plan to divest its remaining interest. Management also noted possible new projects assigned by the Ministry of Public Security.

Overall, the meeting supported a thesis of improving growth momentum and a credible shift toward AI-enabled delivery, but it did not eliminate the central valuation debate. Bookings and AIX activity are strong, yet recognized growth remains below FPT’s historical 20% pace because of APAC weakness, currency translation, cautious traditional spending, and the investment phase of the AI transition. The key proof points for 2H2026 are conversion of signed revenue, delivery of new cybersecurity and legacy-modernization work, sustained AI Factory profitability, and whether FPT can monetize AI productivity without giving the benefits away through pricing.

## 15:35-25:25 — Q&A: pricing models, AI productivity, data-center investment, and share buyback

An analyst from JP Morgan asked whether revenue models were shifting away from seat-based pricing, whether clients demanded a share of AI productivity gains, how much capital FPT planned for data centers, and whether FPT would consider repurchasing shares after the stock-price decline.

Management said the market was moving from traditional named-person time-and-materials contracts toward outcome-based pricing and, especially for new product development where requirements are not known at the start, fixed capacity by sprint. Each sprint can combine human resources and agentic workers. FPT’s strategic ambition is eventually to convert all named-resource T&M work into fixed-capacity or outcome-based formats.

The change is not only about AI. It also lets FPT improve location economics. Mature projects may have operated in Hanoi or Ho Chi Minh City for five to seven years, making senior employees expensive and increasing retention risk. Moving stable work to lower-cost delivery locations can improve profitability while releasing senior staff for more challenging assignments. Large projects worth multiple tens of millions of dollars increasingly use fixed-price or fixed-capacity models. Management argued that FPT’s ability to combine people and agents helped its growth in Japan, where some local systems integrators were less familiar with these models.

On productivity sharing, management said large customers usually have long project backlogs. When FPT raises productivity, clients tend to order more work while maintaining the team rather than cut the workforce. FPT had not seen customers reduce work simply because productivity improved. Some clients, however, were cautious about accelerating spending or signing long contracts because AI models improve rapidly every three to six months and future economics remain uncertain.

At FPT Corporation excluding FPT Telecom, management said the two AI data centers/factories in Vietnam and Japan each involved approximately USD50 million of investment. Both were described as fully booked and operating, with the Vietnam facility contributing materially to domestic IT profit.

Management acknowledged the broad valuation pressure on IT-services shares and foreign outflows from Asian markets. It had discussed a share buyback internally. Protecting shareholder interests was described as a capital-allocation priority, and management said a buyback might be considered at the right time to maximize shareholder value. No amount, authorization, trigger, or timing was provided.

## 65:30-69:30 — Q&A: token costs, model routing, and AI-resource economics

A Templeton analyst asked about annualized token-model cost and how management expected it to evolve. Management said FPT uses multiple models rather than only the most expensive ones. The key is to create reusable AI assets instead of beginning every project from scratch.

FPT Software uses a five-level model-access policy. Employees must demonstrate efficiency with lower-cost models before gaining access to more expensive tools such as Claude Code. FPT also uses open-source and proprietary tools, including CodeVista and TestVista. Several additional model names in the transcript were unclear.

Project budgets combine human-resource cost and token consumption. If a project needs fewer people, the project manager can allocate more budget to tokens. FPT also receives discounts through partnerships with Microsoft and OpenAI and was applying for a regional Anthropic partnership, with management hoping for approval by October 2026.

Management described token consumption as a new production resource alongside human talent. An IT-services provider can route different tasks to different models, choose the best cost-capability combination, and use proprietary models for mature maintenance and operations work where FPT staff already understand the system. This should reduce reliance on expensive tokens.

## 69:30-74:39 — Q&A: AI-related outsourcing services and demand

The final analyst asked what work FPT performs in AI-related outsourcing and whether demand is accelerating. Management divided the offering into two groups.

First, traditional services are shifting to an agentic development life cycle. AI is embedded at each phase to raise productivity and shorten delivery time. The underlying service may remain application development, testing, maintenance, or modernization, but the delivery process is AI-enabled.

Second, AI creates openings in services where FPT historically had less scale, including consulting, business-process outsourcing, and managed services. Large Indian systems integrators and Accenture historically had an advantage because they operated client systems for years and retained the know-how. AI can now reverse engineer source code, read years of project documentation, and build a knowledge database, helping FPT enter these segments. Legacy modernization is a clear example: AI helps FPT understand existing systems by reading code, reducing the incumbency barrier.

Management concluded that both traditional AI-enabled work and newer AI-created services continue to see strong demand, provided FPT can improve productivity, efficiency, and time to delivery. The host then closed the meeting.
